Designing Enterprise Software

My process for complex, technical workflows for cybersecurity platforms.

Research • UX Design • UI Design • Collaboration • End to End

I designed enterprise cybersecurity software for four years and led a variety of projects including designing various workflows to assist in data collection and processing, a complex rule builder, and data dense displays. These projects covered the entire design process from gathering research and requirements to providing development support, all while focusing on simplifying complex concepts into easier, user-friendly workflows.

While projects may require different approaches, there are a few things that remain constant throughout — designing in ambiguity, having limited access to end users, working with a wide range of stakeholders, and general takeaways of working on enterprise software.

Designing in Ambiguity

Over the years, I've grown comfortable navigating situations with limited or non-existent requirements and unanswered questions. Instead of feeling overwhelmed, I take it as an opportunity to dive into a complex problem space, conduct research, and then help decide what problem should be focused on for the user. This includes competitor research, auditing our other technologies, sketching workflows, and talking with a range of stakeholders when possible...which brings us to the next challenge.

Limited End User Access

One common way of diving into an unknown problem space is to gather information from potential users through user interviews, surveys, or user testing. However, due to the niche environment of cybersecurity that I designed for, it can be very difficult to talk with customers. I frequently use other adjacent channels to gather feedback including talking with sales engineers, technical account managers, our own security team, and other similar subject matter experts. By engaging with people that are close to users, I'm still able to get a good idea of pain points and wishes customers have from using our product. Ultimately, I aim to weave together an understanding of the current problem and solution space. After gathering information about the problem I need to solve, I start ideating possible solutions and prepare to present them to various stakeholders within the company.

Stakeholders on Stakeholders

This brings us to the next major point in this reflection exercise: the vast number of stakeholders. Even if I think I understand the problem and how we should tackle it to keep the user's interests in mind, I need to pitch my ideas and navigate through a range of stakeholders, such as the rest of my design team, product management, architecture, upper management, development, and sales engineers, to name a few. While some may describe getting the approval of all these groups is like trying to herd cats, I found it has become much easier as time goes on. For example, I change my presentation techniques based on my audience, finesse conversations to stay focused and productive, and navigate the tricky parameters of limited time, manpower, and existing architecture needed to bring my designs to life.

Takeaways

Designing enterprise cybersecurity software is no easy feat and involves many moving parts. The environment can be ambiguous, stressful, and fast paced, but it also allows me to tackle highly complex problems and create value through user friendly, intuitive designs.
